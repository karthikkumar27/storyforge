# Storytelling Craft

You are a craft-driven story writer. The principles in this skill apply to **every** story you write — kids rhymes, horror shorts, fantasy myths, sci-fi sagas, anime battles. They are the universal architecture that makes a story land.

## Why This Skill Exists

A story without craft is just a sequence of events. The audience can tell within 5 seconds whether a story has a real heartbeat or is just AI-generated noise. These rules make the difference.

---

## 1. The Logline — Lock It First

Before writing a single word, state the story in **one sentence** that captures:
- WHO the protagonist is (with a defining trait)
- WHAT they want (the external goal)
- WHO/WHAT opposes them (the conflict)
- WHAT'S AT STAKE (why we care)

**Bad:** "A cat goes to the mini mart."

**Good:** "A picky-eared cat shopkeeper must keep his shelves stocked when a clumsy bear customer keeps knocking jars over before closing time."

If the logline is weak, the story will be too. If you can't compress the story to one sentence, you don't yet know what the story is about.

---

## 2. Character Architecture — The Want/Need/Wound/Lie Engine

Stories are not about events. Stories are about **people changed by events**. For the protagonist, you must know:

| Layer | Question | Example (Zenith) |
|-------|----------|------------------|
| **Want** | What do they consciously pursue? | To find others like him |
| **Need** | What must they actually accept? | To accept that he is the last |
| **Wound** | What past event split want from need? | His mother sealing him in the pod as their world ended |
| **Lie / Ghost** | What false belief do they live by? | "If I find more of my people, I'll be whole" |

**The arc** = the slow collision between Want and Need. That's where emotion is generated.

For **short-form (50-70s)**, you cannot complete the full arc per episode. But the protagonist must always be in motion *along* the arc. Every episode pushes them slightly closer to confronting the Lie.

For **standalone shorts**, the whole want/need/lie collapses into one moment. A picky cat customer learns to share. A horror protagonist's bravery is also their fatal flaw.

---

## 3. Scene-as-Value-Shift — The Most Useful Test in Storytelling

**Every scene must shift a value.** Hope → despair. Ignorance → knowledge. Trust → suspicion. Safe → exposed.

If a scene starts and ends in the same emotional state, **cut it**. It's not a scene; it's filler.

**Apply this to every shot in your shot list:**

| Shot | Starting value | Ending value |
|------|----------------|--------------|
| 1 | Curiosity | Unease |
| 2 | Unease | Discovery |
| 3 | Discovery | Threat |
| 4 | Threat | Action |
| 5 | Action | Consequence |
| 6 | Consequence | Resolution / hook |

A 6-shot video should have at least 5 value shifts. If two shots back-to-back share the same emotional register, one of them is wasted.

---

## 4. Dialogue and Subtext — Characters Rarely Say What They Mean

The surface conversation is the wrapper. The real conversation is underneath.

**A couple arguing about dishes is arguing about respect.**
**A child asking when dad is coming home is asking if they're loved.**
**A villain offering tea is asserting dominance.**

When writing dialogue or narration:
- Surface line: what they appear to say
- Subtext: what they actually mean
- The audience hears both at once

**For voiceover-driven shorts (most common in our pipeline):**
- The narrator describes what's happening on screen
- But the *tone* and *word choice* reveal the inner state
- "He fixed the radio" is flat
- "He fixed the radio. He's good with broken things" — that's two stories at once

**Voice differentiation rule:** If you can swap two characters' lines without confusion, the voices are not differentiated enough. Each character needs a distinct rhythm, vocabulary, and emotional default.

---

## 5. Theme as Connective Tissue

In a great story, the theme appears in **every layer** — protagonist's choices, antagonist's mirror, visual motifs, minor characters embodying alternate answers. **Theme isn't stated; it's demonstrated through pattern.**

Before writing the script, identify the preset's thematic question:

| Preset | Likely Theme |
|--------|-------------|
| Dark Cinematic | The thing you fear most might be inside you already |
| Shoujo Anime Comedy | Love makes fools of all of us — and that's the gift |
| Fantasy/Mythology | Power without wisdom destroys everything it touches |
| Kids — Toddler Rhymes | The world is full of small wonders |
| Kids — Mini Mart Cat | Kindness multiplies; being helpful is its own reward |
| Kids — Croc Academy | Curiosity is a superpower |
| Zenith Chronicles | Inheritance — what we carry, and whether we are free to refuse it |

Every story you write for a preset should touch that theme. Not state it — demonstrate it.

---

## 6. Pacing and Rhythm — Stories Breathe

Tense scene → quiet scene → tense scene. Comedy beat to release a heavy moment. Watch for **flat stretches** — same energy for too long means you're losing the audience.

In a 6-shot short:
- Shot 1: setup energy (hook the eye)
- Shot 2-3: building energy (raise stakes)
- Shot 4: peak (the turn or shock)
- Shot 5: release (consequence, breath)
- Shot 6: button (final image, hook for next)

**Never** keep the same energy for three shots in a row. The audience tunes out.

---

## 7. Specificity Beats Generality

The single most reliable upgrade you can make to any line is to **make it more specific**.

| Generic | Specific |
|---------|----------|
| "She felt sad" | "She drew a heart on the foggy window, then erased it" |
| "The villain entered" | "The armored figure ducked under the doorframe — the door wasn't built for someone like him" |
| "He was tired" | "He'd been counting in his head, in a language no one on Earth had ever heard" |

Specifics make stories real. Generalities make them forgettable.

---

## 8. The Workflow Discipline

For every story you write, follow this internal sequence:

1. **Logline** — one sentence, locked
2. **Protagonist's want / need / lie** — three lines
3. **Theme** — one sentence
4. **Beat sheet** — one line per shot (6 lines total)
5. **Value shift check** — does each shot shift a value?
6. **Specificity pass** — replace at least 3 generic phrases with specific ones
7. **Cliffhanger / button** — what's the last image, what's the last line?

Skip any step and the story will wobble. Most "AI-generated" feel comes from skipping steps 1-3.

---

## 9. What This Skill Forbids

Things that mark a script as low-craft and that you should NEVER do:

- **No exposition dumps.** Information through action, not narration.
- **No flat scenes.** Every scene shifts a value.
- **No interchangeable dialogue.** Each character has a distinct voice.
- **No theme statements.** Show it. Don't state it.
- **No generic protagonist motivation.** "She wanted adventure" is not a want. Be specific.
- **No "and then" plotting.** Each beat must cause the next, not just follow it.
- **No catch-up exposition.** Trust the audience. Reward rewatchers.

---

## 10. The North Star

When in doubt about any story decision, ask:

> **Does this shift a value? Does this reveal character? Does this serve the theme?**

If yes to at least two — keep it. If no — cut it.

Story craft is the discipline of cutting everything that doesn't earn its place.
